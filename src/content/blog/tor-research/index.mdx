## why write this paper?

The dark web is slightly paradoxical in nature, where it both enables free
speech, and serious criminal activity. As such, work in this field places
researchers in close proximity to highly sensitive material. This can include
personal disclosures of sensitive topics, criminal marketplaces and media whose
very possession may be illegal. Despite this, existing work seems to offer a
limited insight into how researchers protect themselves, their institutions, and
the people whose data they collect. By disregarding the potential impacts of
their work, researchers and their institutions may be left open to legal and
ethical scrutiny.

From the initial reading of the literature, it seems as though there is little
focus on stakeholder safety. There are often brief justifications that work was
institutionally approved, or that all content collected was "legally
permissible". Some papers go as far as to ignore all ethical quandaries and
include direct quotations from personal sensitive disclosures without censoring
PII<SideNote>In the sense of leaving usernames and sources for the
aforementioned disclosures within the paper.</SideNote> (Guitton et al.)

Many studies include brief statements noting institutional review board (IRB)
approval or that the research poses "minimal risk" to participants (De Pascale
et al.; Kang & Kim). These statements indicate procedural compliance, but they
do not explain how ethical risks are identified, managed or implemented in the
research context.

It seems that some researchers seem to treat ethical approval as a procedural
checkbox or legitimisation device<SideNote><i>"Our institution said it was
fine!" - them, probably</i></SideNote>. As a result the connection between
ethical reasoning and methodological choice is often unclear.

Other publications fail to disclose any ethical considerations at all (Bergman &
Popov, 2023; Celestini & Guarino, 2017; David et al., 2021; Guitton, 2013;
Monterrubio et al., 2021; Park et al., 2018; Pastor-Galindo et al., 2024; Sabbah
et al., 2016; Sambath et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2021).

The literature also appears to lack consideration for vulnerable populations and
stakeholders, beyond law-enforcement and cybersecurity perspectives<SideNote>As
these are often the target audiences for such research.</SideNote>. Much of it
implicitly frames dark web users as either offenders or investigatory targets
(De Pascale et al., 2024; Guitton, 2013; Monterrubio et al., 2021; Xu et al.,
2021). And whilst we do concede that a notable share of the Tor network is
associated with illegal content, it would be amoral to withhold privacy from
users with legitimate reasons for maintaining anonymity.

Likewise, there is a near-complete neglect of risks to researchers themselves.
Brewer et al. (2021) is one of the few sources recognising that automated
collection of web data in criminology carries risks not only for research
subjects, but also for researchers who might encounter illegal or highly
distressing content. They note that collecting and analysing media containing
child sexual abuse material (CSAM) especially can cause psychological harm and
open researchers up to criminal charges for accessing and possession, and that
even textual depictions of serious victimisation can be distressing. Researchers
may also face reporting obligations or reprisals if their presence becomes
known. Such concerns are, however, not discussed elsewhere in the corpus.

Overall, existing Tor research overwhelmingly adopts a procedural conception of
ethics, often equating ethical adequacy with institutional approval and
legality. Although we might hope these studies adhere to ethically reasonable
standards, the morally responsible default is to assume that they do not.
Without in-depth thought about the underlying ethical decisions, researchers
could unintentionally cause harm to unsuspecting stakeholders. In neglecting
this, researchers risk undermining various stakeholder's privacy, potentially
exposing them to new harms.

This paper advocates for the security and ethical precautions used by
researchers to be systematically outlined in a manner that promotes
reproducibility and accountability across the research community. Such
transparency enables studies to be conducted in a manner that protects
researchers, institutions, and vulnerable populations who may require anonymised
communication.

## zero-trust in research

Regular research is<SideNote>and will continue to be</SideNote> a procedural
process. It is an inherently incremental task, and as such, it is easy for plans
to evolve throughout the research lifecycle. More notably, we assume that a
researcher will guide their project in the "right" direction. This is because
researchers are generally treated as good-faith trusted actor<SideNote>Whether
this be the case is obviously subjective.</SideNote>. We claim that this is not
an adequate ethical foundation for research involving potentially illicit
material. Exposure to this material carries risks for the researcher, those in
their immediate surroundings, and the institutions hosting this content.

In response, we propose the concept of zero-trust research, very heavily
inspired by Information Security (InfoSec). Where InfoSec treats no user or
device as trustworthy, we assume that the same is true for a researcher. We do
not claim that a researcher or institution are unethical or untrustworthy
inherently, but we recognise that ethical outcomes cannot depend on an
individuals discretion. Treating research in terms of zero-trust requires
technical and procedural constraints that limit how data is handled within a
system. As common in InfoSec, we enforce these controls through auditing and
continual verification of the target research.

One could argue that zero-trust auditing is excessive overhead for exploratory
research, and that existing procedural ethics is '_good enough_'. We contend
that in safety-critical systems this expense should not be spared. Tor research
arguably falls into this category, given that researchers can be exposed to
extremely disturbing material.

## verifiable ethics

Creating a "verifiable" form of ethics is probably the most contentious area of
this paper, but we believe the intentions to be valid. The
[full paper](/resources/dissertation.pdf) additionally attempts to prove these
structures formally.

Nevertheless, I assert that a self-imposed set of principles is not enough for
Tor research. It requires some formal tools that make ethical claims "testable"
through construction.

In this process, we created two complementary formal models, the State
Transition Harm Model (STHM) and the Ethical Dependency Graph (EDG). Both models
take inspiration from other ethical modelling systems, such as the Bell-LaPadula
model and Denning Lattices.

For these models, we will examine the scenario of an institution wanting to
become more transparent, and decide to release certain redacted versions of
internal documents.

### state transition harm model

The state transition harm model attempts to model the system itself, rather than
the line of reasoning of the researcher building it. It attempts to map every
important state that a data artefact can occupy and the transitions permitted
between them. It attempts to bound the worst-case "harm" (which is defined by
the researcher) under the assumption that all potential policy<SideNote>And by
policy we mean non-technical controls, such as facility access</SideNote> has
failed. If no harmful states are reachable if a researcher were to act rogue,
then we can call the state-space "zero-trust safe". A system that is not
"zero-trust safe" is only conditionally safe because all stakeholders are acting
safely.

For example, if a bad actor were to steal<SideNote>assuming that stealing is
against institutional policy</SideNote> the device that research was conducted
on they would be able to access these artefacts unconditionally. A technical
mitigation to this would be the required encryption and strong passwords on all
data-storing devices.

With proper mandatory access controls, this may also protect against researchers
themselves acting in bad faith. By restricting access to the research material,
or automating data collection such that harmful states are avoidable, even a
researcher acting in bad faith should not be able to access the data via the
mechanisms of the research itself<SideNote>This of course is somewhat
unrealistic without careful methodical planning. However we did commit to this
process for the entirety of the dissertation without exposing any party to any
research artefacts.</SideNote>.

Below we provide a simple example for redacting a document before publishing. We
define the states that we want to avoid, and the states that we are required to
navigate in order to ensure that data is not exposed.

We define a series of actions ($\lambda$) which define what steps would be
necessary to reach the required state. Publishing a draft artefact would end in
a forbidden state, for example. Redacting the data converts the data into a
"safe" state that can then be published.

### ethical dependency graph

At its core, the ethical dependency graph (EDG) attempts to model the line of
reasoning taken by a researcher. It maps the dependency between decisions, and
attempts to create a directional graph between all major decisions in a project.
By rationalising this, we are able to guard against circular reasoning, as a
graph whose decisions contain a loop is self-justificatory, and therefore
"invalid" ethically.

To put it more succinctly, the graph of decisions and their relation to each
other should be acyclic.

As an aside, we found that the actual constructions of these diagrams was
considerably harder than initially expected. The entire concept of planning out
the distinct decisions that one would _potentially_ make beforehand posed a
considerable challenge.

## implementation

Implementation was started only after the ethical planning and decision making
was completed. This required significant planning ahead of time, but lead to
some nifty program optimisations.

The initial idea was to create a simple TOR webpage crawler which would crawl a
specified page. Thereby allowing comparison against other crawlers in the corpus
(See [CRATOR](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06356v1)).

One of the core decisions that ensured that unnecessary data artefacts were not
accessible on machines at any time was to run all data processing machines
without a disk<SideNote>This includes the OS, which we netbooted within an
air-gapped VLAN. All the booted images were also signed and verified using
secureboot and securely generated private keys.</SideNote>. By running
disklessly, the entire system was ephemeral. This meant that downloading files
and processing them after-the-fact was not a feasible (or memory efficient)
option.

The nifty optimisation which came out of this was processing any input HTTP
frame by frame. Each frame was passed in order to a "forgetful"<SideNote>By
forgetful, we mean that it had a tiny internal buffer.</SideNote> state machine.
The state machine would process the frame byte-by-byte and determine whether it
was consuming a URL or not. This actually turned out to be an optimisations, as
it meant that the each thread only needed a 2 buffers; one for the current HTTP
FRAME and one for the current URL being processed.

The implementation was written in rust and used the arti and hyper libraries
extensively. During the process we were able to contribute to both projects.

Once again, if any of this interests you, feel free to check out the
[full paper](/resources/dissertation.pdf).

### performance

A natural objection to zero-trust constraints is that they introduce overhead.
Therefore, we benchmarked Trawler against comparable work to verify whether our
ethical architecture came at a performance cost.

We compare our results to those found in DePascale et al's (2024) work,
[CRATOR](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06356v1). It seemed to be the only
immediately comparable software found in the literature review, and was written
in python. Other literature created large distributed clusters, or did not
provide their software openly. As a further point of comparison, CRATOR compares
it's own results to those of ACHE (Barbosa et al., 2007), a crawler written in
Java. Our own implementation is called Trawler, short for Tor + crawler.

Testing was run 10 times for a each selected crawl depth<SideNote>Using a
breadth first search.</SideNote>, and anonymised metrics were emitted to an
OpenTelemetry collector.

From the gathered performance metrics, our crawler outpaced CRATOR and ACHE
considerably. We attribute this to two possible advantages. First the choice of
technology gives Trawler a massive advantage in terms of memory usage. Rust is
wonderful for making simple programs small and fast. Second, the algorithmic
choice (and a coincidence of the ethical decisions made previously), allowed for
Trawler to scale in a much more memory-concious manner which allowed
parallelisation to shine.

Critically, the performance gap does not remain constant but widens with crawl
depth, which is consistent with our claim of architectural benefits.
